"Can I learn Japanese from anime?" is probably the question Japanese learners ask most. The honest answer: **yes, but not through "watching" as such — through lots of input you partly understand, plus a bit of active effort.** People who've followed anime with subtitles in their own language for ten years often understand little more than ありがとう and 大丈夫: their eyes were reading their native language the whole time, and the Japanese was background noise.

The method that actually works is called **immersion**: watching and listening to content made by native speakers for native speakers, with Japanese itself as your main way of following the story. This page follows the route in the [immersion guide](/immersion), from zero to learning while you watch. It uses Japanese as the example; other languages work the same way.

## What actually makes learning Japanese from anime work?

You can read this paragraph effortlessly not because you memorised grammar rules, but because years of massive input in your native language built up countless intuitions about it. A foreign language is no different: **intuition only comes from large amounts of real input.** Textbooks and grammar books lay the foundation, but language is vast — far bigger than any textbook can cover.

As input material, anime has some natural advantages:

- **You want to watch it anyway.** Immersion has just one requirement: genuine interest in the content. Drilling vocabulary and doing exercises is hard to keep up, but following a show you love takes no "resolve".
- **The picture helps you understand.** For lines you can't follow, the scene, facial expressions and actions often give away half the meaning.
- **The spoken language is real.** Speed, connected speech, tone and ellipsis are all real Japanese, the kind textbooks don't teach.

Know its limits too: anime Japanese contains a lot of exaggerated character speech and genre-specific vocabulary (battle shows especially), so don't watch only one genre. And if you only listen without looking anything up, many words stay "heard but not understood" forever. That's why you still need a small active part: looking up words and making cards.

## Step 0 from zero: set up your tools

[Download Fushi](/download) and follow the onboarding wizard: dictionaries, the word-audio library, and downloading and connecting Anki. The [recommended pack](/faq/pack.en) in the wizard bundles common Japanese dictionaries, pitch accent, frequency and pronunciation audio, so you don't have to hunt for resources.

Once that's done, tapping a word in the subtitles looks it up, and one more tap on the plus makes an Anki card with the original sentence, a screenshot and the original audio. Connecting Anki is covered in [How do I connect Anki?](/faq/anki.en), and your first card in [How do I mine cards while watching anime?](/faq/first-card.en)

Where to get anime: import videos you already have; connect a media server such as Jellyfin / Emby / Plex if you run one (see [Media servers](/faq/media-servers.en)); on every platform except iOS you can also search for a show under **Browse → Discover** and download it inside the app (see [Should I stream anime or download it?](/faq/anime-download.en)). Following App Store rules, the iOS version has no Discover page and no download centre — use local files, a media server, or [Interconnect](/faq/interconnect.en) with another device.

## Step 1: learn the kana

There's no way around the Japanese kana. You don't need to know them perfectly — **just get through hiragana first**. You'll meet them constantly in subtitles and lookups, and they'll become more familiar on their own. Katakana can be picked up as you go.

Any tool will do: the immersion guide recommends a practice site that unlocks the kana step by step and trains typing at the same time, or use whatever method you like. This step usually takes a few days to a couple of weeks.

## Step 2: basic vocabulary and grammar — and start watching

Once you know the kana, build a foundation with Anki decks. The immersion guide recommends **Kaishi 1.5k** (a beginner vocabulary deck of about 1,500 words) and **おにぎり文法** (a grammar deck; going up to about N3/N4 is enough).

Two pieces of advice:

- **5–20 new cards a day is plenty.** Anki's review load builds up after two or three weeks, and too many new cards is why most people give up on Anki. Switch the scheduler to FSRS and set desired retention somewhere between 70 and 80% and the review pressure drops considerably — see [How do I use Anki?](/faq/anki-basics.en) and [How many new cards a day?](/faq/cards-per-day.en)
- **Don't wait until you've finished the decks to start watching.** While you're studying vocabulary, start the next step: immersion. Many people feel they aren't "ready" and want to study enough first — that never works. However much you prepare, you won't understand everything the first time you face real anime.

## Step 3: watch, look up words, mine cards

This is the main track. Watch shows you like with **Japanese subtitles**, tap words you don't know to look them up, and tap the plus on the ones worth remembering. You don't need to look up every word or mine every word you look up — **being able to keep watching matters more than looking up everything.**

At first there's a stretch where you understand almost nothing. That's normal; nearly everyone goes through it. Accept that you can't understand everything — the more ambiguity you can tolerate, the faster you improve. A few things that make this stretch easier:

- **Start with easy content**: slice-of-life is easier than battle or mystery shows; works whose dialogue is mostly everyday conversation are the best starting point.
- **Spoilers first**: read a plot summary before watching, or rewatch a show you've already seen in your own language — when you know the plot, all your attention can go to the Japanese.
- **Native-language subtitles as a safety net, not the main act**: keeping them on all the time is usually not recommended, but when you're completely lost, hold out without them for a while and reveal them briefly when you can't. Fushi's secondary subtitle can keep a translation blurred until you need it — see [How do I use a secondary subtitle as a bridge?](/faq/secondary-subtitle.en); which subtitles to use at each stage is covered in [Japanese or native-language subtitles?](/faq/subtitle-language.en)

Which sentences are worth turning into cards, and why cards should carry the original sentence and audio, is explained in [What is sentence mining?](/faq/sentence-mining.en)

## How much time per day? How long until I understand?

**15 to 30 minutes of Anki a day**, done properly, beats an occasional two-hour session — habit matters more than intensity. On a bad day, five minutes still counts; what matters most is not breaking the chain. Watching time is the more the better, because input volume is the foundation — and it happens to be time you'd gladly spend anyway.

As for "how long until I understand": it varies with your daily input, the difficulty of what you watch and how much Japanese you've been exposed to before. There's no number anyone can promise. A more practical way to measure is to watch for changes: the day you suddenly catch a word you just studied, the episode where you follow most of the plot without tapping anything — that's progress. Forgetting is normal; fighting it is what Anki is for. If it won't stick today, it will one day.

## Common questions

- **I have zero interest in learning kana or vocabulary**: normal — almost everyone feels that way. You don't have to wait until you "like" it; five minutes a day is fine. Progress itself brings motivation.
- **I only want to understand, not to write**: absolutely fine. The immersion route is mostly listening and reading, and Fushi's default cards test "see the word, recall its reading and meaning", not handwriting.
- **I'm learning a different language, not Japanese**: that works too. Fushi supports many target languages and the route is the same; step 1 becomes that language's script basics and the starter decks become ones for that language. The recommended pack is Japanese — skip it and download dictionaries by "learning language" in dictionary management instead; see [Is it only for Japanese?](/faq/languages.en)
